The problem isn’t AI. It’s AI without guidance.

September 2026 · Secondary-school trial

Structured AI revision shows promise.

A four-week randomized Medly trial in England found higher science scores than self-directed revision (Hedges’ g = 0.33; 95% CI 0.18–0.48).

Preprint. Of 929 baseline students, 644 completed post-tests. Attrition was 30.7%; tests were curriculum-aligned, not standardized.

October 2026 · University trial

Course access alone is not enough.

A randomized study of 2,379 undergraduates found lower final grades in its same-course comparison when an AI study assistant was offered.

Working paper. Most instructors used direct-answer mode; uptake was about 15%. This does not establish the effect of scaffolded tutoring or K–12 use.

relative exam performance, unassisted
Peer-reviewed · High-school mathematics

Better practice scores can hide weaker learning.

In a trial with nearly 1,000 high-school students, unrestricted GPT-4 help improved practice performance but lowered later unaided exam scores by 17% relative to control.

A tutor using teacher-designed hints largely removed that loss, but did not significantly outperform control on the exam.

February 2026 · Faculty survey

Faculty are concerned about original thinking.

In College Board’s faculty survey, 84% agreed that AI reduces students’ ability to express original ideas. Another item found the same share concerned about deep engagement.

These are faculty perceptions, not a measured causal effect on students. Respondents taught in higher education.

March 2026 · Student survey

Concern is rising alongside use.

RAND found 67% believed AI use for schoolwork harms critical thinking, up from 54%. Concern was higher among nonusers than users.

A belief about students generally, not a measured decline in respondents’ own skills.

Most surveyed students already use it.

Reported homework use rose from 48% to 62% between May and December 2025.

RAND’s survey covered students aged 12–29 across middle school, high school, and college. Use is not a measure of dependence.

The synthesis

The design matters. So does the evidence.

Learning outcomesIndependent workTeacher involvementLocal evaluation

Our reading: AI access alone is not a learning strategy. Test how it is used and what students can do independently. OECD’s 2026 review likewise emphasizes teaching goals, productive effort, and rigorous evaluation.

OECD Digital Education Outlook · January 2026 ↗

These studies evaluate other tools and settings, not Orchen. They inform our design; our own pilots must establish results.

Three responses to this moment
✗

Ban it.

Sets a boundary, but may move use to personal tools. Pair restrictions with clear guidance.

✗

Ignore it.

Creates a growing gap between what students submit and what they actually understand.

→

Design it.

Set teaching goals, involve teachers, and check learning with the AI put away.

The question for a pilot: what can students do on their own after learning with AI?
